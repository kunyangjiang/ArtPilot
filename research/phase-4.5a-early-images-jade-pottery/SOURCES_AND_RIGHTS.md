Altamira photos: **the photographed location cannot be verified.** The museum's file descriptions name the motif
(e.g. "Máscara en la Galería Final") but do not say whether the photograph was taken in the original cave or in the
replica (Neocueva). An earlier draft inferred "original cave" from the Galería Final subjects; that inference is not
established (the replica's extent was not checked) and has been withdrawn. Every Altamira caption now says the location
is not stated; the identification of the motifs, the photographer and the CC BY-SA 3.0 licence (VRT ticket) are
unaffected.

Claims and sources (text only, no images):

- Altamira dating: CENIEH note on García-Diez et al., *J. Archaeol. Sci.* 40 (2013) 4098–4106 (≥20,000 years, 35,000–15,200
  years ago, minimum ages from calcite); Pike et al., *Science* 336 (2012) (method; minimum ages); Valladas et al., *Nature*
  357 (1992) 68–70 (bison charcoal 14,000 ± 400 BP). The lesson states that the radiocarbon figure is uncalibrated and
  that calendar age is somewhat older; no calibrated figure is given.
- Discovery: University of Cantabria page (1868 cave found by Modesto Cubillas; 1879 paintings noticed; 1880 publication;
  1902 Cartailhac, "Mea culpa d'un sceptique"). Altamira as the first Palaeolithic parietal art identified scientifically:
  CENIEH.
- Bison size, use of rock bulges, low ceiling: a Complutense University lecture (one source; stated as such).
- Blombos Cave: Henshilwood et al., *Nature* 562 (2018) 115–118 (ochre crosshatch on a silcrete flake from ~73,000-year-old
  levels: **a layer date**).
- Sulawesi: Oktaviana et al., *Nature* (2024), Leang Karampuang, figurative scene ≥51,200 years (LA-U-series minimum age);
  Oktaviana et al., "Rock art from at least 67,800 years ago in Sulawesi", *Nature* 650: 652 (published online 21 Jan.
  2026, open access), doi:10.1038/s41586-025-09968-y. **Checked against the original abstract (2026-10-26):** LA-U-series
  dating of calcite overlying a hand stencil at Liang Metanduno, Muna Island, gave 71.6 ± 3.8 ka, "providing a
  minimum-age constraint of 67.8 ka for the underlying motif". The lesson gives the calcite date and the minimum age, and
  says it is neither the stencil's exact age nor the beginning of image-making. The paper compares it with the Spanish
  hand stencil attributed to Neanderthals (a 1.1 kyr greater minimum).
- Spanish "Neanderthal" dates (Hoffmann et al., *Science* 2018, >64,800 years) presented as contested; the critiques are
  not cited individually.
- No hypothesis about purpose (hunting magic, shamanism, storytelling, marking) is presented as proven.

Claims and sources:

- **Observed:** square-outside, round-inside form; tiers; on one tier, raised bands with fine lines, a shallow circle, an
  oval bar with incised curls (Met high-resolution image; find-game boxes set by eye). The Met's own texts call the bar a
  mouth (overview) and a nose (audio guide); the lesson says so.
- **Museum statements:** "enigmatic in function and meaning … probably signified wealth and social status"; weight 8–10 lb;
  commonly found in tombs (Met 2004.52 and audio). Inner cylinders bored with a tubular drill, probably bamboo (AIC
  1950.526, stated as the AIC's view). Liangzhu perforations drilled from two sides leaving a ridge halfway (J. K. Wilson,
  Freer essay).
- **Material:** nephrite shaped by abrasion with sand harder than jade (Britannica "Chinese jade"; National Palace Museum
  exhibition text). Mohs ~6–6.5 is given as approximate.
- **Excavation record:** Caoxieshan 1973; Sidun 1978–82; Fanshan 1986 (11 large tombs with strict differences); Yaoshan 1987
  (altar, 12 tombs); Fanshan M12 complete "god emblem" image; Liangzhu culture first identified from black pottery in 1936
  (Liu Bin, Freer essay, 2016). Socially graded cemeteries, ca. 3300–2300 BCE (UNESCO WHC 1592).
- **Interpretation, labelled:** "god emblem" as researchers' name and consensus reading; Liu Bin's derivation of the cong form
  from the emblem (one excavator's view); possible link to Shang "taotie" (Julia K. Murray's 1982 curatorial remark on
  F1916.511; "still debated").
- **Re-dating case:** Freer F1916.511 attributed to Xia, Late Zhou, Western Zhou (1964), changed to Neolithic (1980–82) by
  comparison with excavated pieces (Freer object record).
- **Banshan jar:** coiling, paddling, painting before firing, mostly from burials (Minneapolis Institute of Art and
  University of Michigan Museum of Art records for comparable jars, summarised). **No cemetery-level statistic was found;
  the lesson says so rather than claiming painted pottery was common to all graves.** The Met audio guide misdates this jar
  to the second millennium BCE; the lesson uses the object record's date.
- **Later text:** *Zhouli*, Chunguan, Da zongbo, "以苍璧礼天，以黄琮礼地", presented as a much later text, not evidence for
  Liangzhu use.
