Claims and sources:

- Intarsia, latticework cabinets and linear perspective, objects reflecting Federico's interests and library, the
  helmet with an eagle, the caged bird, attribution, the similar studiolo still in situ at Urbino, the Latin
  inscription (partly lost), provenance: Met 39.153 (overview, audio guide, inscription and provenance tabs).
- 66-inch horizon for a standing viewer, fixed viewing distance and centre point; moldings showing tops or bottoms
  according to the eye; fifteen woods (bog oak for shadows, spindlewood and boxwood for highlights); shoulder knife,
  planes, fretsaws; moved via Rome and Venice; repeated restorations; Luigi Rizzo's note of September 1877; acquired
  1939, shown 1941–67, stored, restored 1996: Daniel Kershaw, "On the Gubbio Studiolo", Met Perspectives (adapted from
  *Points of View*). **Olga Raggio's view that the eye height matches the duke's own height is presented as one
  scholar's opinion.**
- Floor: the Met's record of 350 earthenware tiles made in 1995, on view in Gallery 501, numbered as an installation
  ("Inst."). **That these are the studiolo's floor is an inference from the record and the photograph, stated as such.**
- Netsuke, ojime and inrō worn on a cord under the obi, the netsuke over the sash as a counterweight; tobacco, medicine,
  seals; townsmen's use in the Edo period; durable materials (wood, ivory) for daily wear; 19th-century Western
  collecting; almost one thousand netsuke at the Met (2014); the Kiyonaga print as an example of netsuke worn at the
  obi: Anabelle Gambert-Jouan, "Celebrating #NetsukeNovember on Twitter", Met Perspectives, 2014. **The round object
  on the attendant's sash is not identified in detail; the lesson says so.**
- Ci wara: tribute to the culture hero Ci Wara; seasonal paired masquerades in a historically agrarian society;
  crests worn by dancers; fusion of roan antelope, anteater (the Met's word) and pangolin; choreography of tilling;
  fibre costume evoking rain; male headdress referring to the sun; Bamana sculptors as blacksmiths (numu): Met
  2016.574. **When and how this headdress left Mali is not recorded; the lesson says the performance description is
  general, not specific to this piece.**
- Earlier ArtPilot courses are referred to only in one sentence each (bronze vessels, course 8; Greek sculpture,
  course 11; Gothic cathedral, course 30; Fan Kuan's hanging scroll, course 1).

| Representative work, card, homepage feature | Hippopotamus ("William") | 17.9.1 (544227) | ca. 1961–1878 BCE, Middle Kingdom, Dynasty 12 (Senwosret I–Senwosret II) | Faience; L. 20, W. 7.5, H. 11.2 cm | Meir, tomb B3 of the nomarch Senbi II, pit 1 (steward Senbi); excavated by Sayyid Pasha Khashaba, May 1910; his share in the division of finds; Maurice Nahman, Cairo, Nov. 1910; Dikran Kelekian, 1911; purchased 1917, gift of Edward S. Claims and sources, by strength as the lesson presents them:

- **Documented:** findspot, one of a pair, Senbi's burial equipment (canopic box, coffin, boat and food-production
  models), provenance chain: Met 17.9.1.
- **Observed:** blue faience body with lotus buds, flowers and leaves painted under the glaze; one leg differs in
  colour and sheen from the other three (visible in DP247657; the find-game box was placed against that photograph).
- **Material evidence:** only the front left leg is original; neck and three legs restored (white UV fluorescence of a
  modern adhesive); XRF showed a restored surface even under the surviving leg; copper in the glaze, manganese in the
  painted plants; a fine quartz layer under the glaze: Serotta, Riccardelli & Schorsch, "Getting to Know 'William'",
  Met Perspectives, 2017. The restorations are left visible on purpose: Catharine Roehrig, Met audio guide.
  **The UV, X-ray and lab photographs in that article carry no open licence and are not reproduced.**
- **Interpretation (supported):** lotus plants as the marsh habitat; lotus and the hippo's surfacing as symbols of
  regeneration; faience hippos as grave goods meant to give regenerative power: Met 17.9.1 and Isabel Stünkel,
  "Hippopotami in Ancient Egypt", Heilbrunn Timeline, 2017.
- **Inference (weaker, stated with "probably"):** legs purposely broken so the animal could not harm the dead: Met
  17.9.1 ("probably"), Stünkel ("probably broken off deliberately" for many such statuettes). Supporting context:
  hippos as dangerous (boats, a medical recipe for bites, the papyrus line on the harvest), images believed able to
  come alive: Stünkel. **Accidental damage is not excluded; the lesson says no record settles it for this piece.**
- **Alternative reading:** hippos as icons of the royal hippo hunt, cross bands read as ropes; Stünkel's suggestion that
  bands, like broken legs, may control the dangerous aspect: Stünkel.
- **Comparative evidence:** nearly one hundred faience hippos survive, most without known context; a Met hippo (26.7.898)
  recorded as found in the wrappings of Reniseneb's mummy: Stünkel.
- **Modern reception:** the name "William" from a 1931 *Punch* story, republished in the Met's Bulletin the same year:
  Met 17.9.1. His 100th year at the Met (2017): Serotta et al. The curator's remark that his eyes "are not happy":
  Roehrig, audio guide.
- Further reading cited by Stünkel (Müller 1975; Behrmann 1989–96) is listed but **not read directly**.
